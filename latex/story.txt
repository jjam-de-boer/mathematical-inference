1.b Introduction
Setting the mission within Jaynes [JAYNES]:
Proper process of adding new knowledge to existing information -- which should be the process of science.
There are three aspects to the mathematical and statistical sciences (Jaynes does not consider anything outside of these disciplines, like existential philosophy, French literature in the 17th century or the efficient management of large cooperations, e.g.) that Jaynes deemed essential:
- Solid foundations for mathematics
- Solid foundations for statistics
- A good and honest attitude for practicing these sciences
According to Jaynes there is a lot wrong with all three aspects. The sense of urgency and passion can be felt throughout his work [see quote Huizinga]. He flags his discontent with all three aspects, he proposes a solution for statistics (a universal, subjective Bayesian probability theory); the other two are discussed, but not solved. 

This work is aimed to addresses all three things in a holistic view. The first two are tackled using Martin-Löf type theory, a form of mathematics Jaynes surely would have approved of. Jaynes' solution for statistics is scrutinized and problems with its derivation are flagged and addressed using this Martin-Löf type theory; furthermore, his theory is extended to more deeply include subjectivity and also account for Pearl's causal framework. The third problem is placed in a much wider context and a practical proposal is made with a combination of policies, regulations and incentives. 

There is a dire need to consider these parts together: As mentioned by [JAYNES and MCELREATH] inference is only one component of scientific knowledge production; AI systems that formalize inference inherit the weaknesses of the wider scientific pipeline in which questions, data, models, incentives, publication, and review are produced. It is therefore extremely important that both the fomralization of mathematical inference, as well as the environment in which those inferences take place, are sensible and constructive. If not, the inferences will only be as good as the weakest link.

Here, Jaynes' claims are investigated further.
2.a Solid foundations for mathematics
    - Axiomatic set theory, also known as Zermelo-Fraenkel+axiom of Choice (ZFC) theory, which is prevalent in modern mathematics lends itself too easily for sloppy work in Jaynes' view. This can be seen by the abundance of mathematical paradoxes, especially concerning infinity, like Banach-Tarski, Borel-Kolmogorov, Ross-Littlewood (or likewise, Thomson's lamp) [INFINITE PARADOXING]. Jaynes views this 'infinite paradoxing' a degredation of mathematical quality and rigor, as he prefers a way of doing mathematics where one starts from finite quantities and then only in limiting processes go to infinity -- a way of doing mathematics more in line of Gauss, Kronecker and Pioncaré rather than Cantor, Hilbert and Nicolas Bourbaki/the Bourbaki group. It is not so much wrong (although sometimes it is), but it is more that a lot of energy and effort is wasted on mathematical theories that sound profound, but are from a pragmatic standpoint absolutely worthless -- and doing experimental and pragmatic science is in Jaynes' view essential for the progress of science (which is not suprising given he is a physicist) [see quote Jaynes].
    - He flags the problem, but doesn't solve it; in fact, in his work he resorts to axiomatic set theory as a basis for his theory.

2.b Solid foundations for statistics
    - This field of research takes the heaviest blow in Jaynes' work. He critizes it for being unstructured, erratic, illogical and inconsistent. He provides ample examples of statistics that seem arbitrary and derivations that are rather hand-waving rather than rigorous. Clear examples of this can be seen in experiments where multiple teams versed in mathematical statistics receive the same data set, but arrive at opposite conclusions [CONTRADICTING CLAIMS]. This is a serious accusation and threatens the very foundations of every statistical claim that has ever been made in economy, psychology, sociology and other sciences [REPRODUCTION CRISIS]. This is even more serious than previous point, in the sense that not only time and energy is wasted, but it actually poses a serious and accute risk of propagating incorrect conclusions, which is directly contrary to doing science.
    - He solves it by proposing a universal, subjective Bayesian probability theory, based on logic -- internally sound and consistent, inspired by the works of Cox and Jeffreys. He uses it to solve some problems that cannot be easily or consistently solved in mathematical statistics. This is absolutely the main feature that is achieved by Jaynes in his work and a contribution to scientific praxis that cannot be easily overstated. His solution is not without problems, but an important and sincere contribution to the advancement of science.

2.c A good and honest attitude for doing these sciences
    - Jaynes makes rather personal attacks and accusations on people doing mathematics and statistics in a rather `unscientific' way. Especially Fisher, but also Feller, the Nicolas Bourbaki/the Bourbaki group and others take personal blows. He has a rather strong opinion on that science should not be primarily seen as the process for doing 'useless' or contrived things and/or boasting someone's career, but as a helpful instrument for making mathematical inferences in the real world [again, see quote Jaynes]. Jaynes objects to their method on grounds of:
        - gamesmanship: showing off rather than contributing something significant
        - cutting corners: delivering something (generally impressive-looking), but without rigor
        - hidden assumptions: sweeping the essential, but illogical or incorrect, foundations of a theory under the carpet 
        - hyperfocused: delivering results that are not focused on the larger narrative, and are sometimes completely contradictory to it
        - theoretical: delivering results that are not applicable to any real-world design, nor have the faintest ambition to be in accordance with the experimental sciences
    - He flags it, but doesn't solve it, except by public shaming; in fact, in his work one could definitely accuse Jaynes himself of cutting corners [not so much of the other points], as will be seen in the next movement.

From the points above it can be seen that mathematics, statistics as well as the scientific attitude of its main and most influential contributors is not progressing science in a meaningful way.

3.a Jaynes' + Cox theory, what is derived
Inspired by Cox and Jeffreys and discontent by (then) current scientific praxis, Jaynes claimed to have found a universal mathematical, yet subjective theory for adding new knowledge to existing information -- either for certain information (propositional logic) or for uncertain information (a calculus of probability, also known as: ``Probability theory''.). This was done by adding suggestive rules as extension of propositional logic, by which the rules for probability theory could be derived (e.g.: Kolmogorov's axioms for probability theory can be derived from these). The rules are:
1. Divisibility and comparability. Plausibility is represented by a real number that varies continuously with the information available.
2. Consistency. Whenever a plausibility can be derived by two independent routes, the two results must coincide.
3. Common–sense. Classical Aristotelian logic should emerge as the special case in which every proposition is known to be either true or false; plausibilities must change smoothly with the plausibilities of their premises.
These rules lead to the familiar axioms of probability theory:
1. Non-negativity. For any event A ∈ F : P (A) ≥ 0
2. Normalization. The probability of the entire sample space Ω is exactly 1: P (Ω) = 1
3. Countable Additivity. For any countable sequence of mutually exclusive (disjoint) events A 1 , A 2 , A 3 , · · · ∈ F (where A i ∩ A j = ; for all i ̸= j ): Ã ! ∞ ∞ X [ P (A i ) P Ai = i =1 i =1 
He makes the claims that thus everything not following these rules, contradicts logic/the process of science. Jaynes is not the first to use Bayesian theory [KEYNES, JEFFREYS, COX, RAMSEY], but the first to derive it, relatively thoroughly, and to give an account on how rules directly lead to another framework for making inferences and how it structurally differs from the frequentists' approach.

Finding many examples in his book, Jaynes makes a quite convincing point that mathematical statistics as practiced so far, is often not following these rules (have a slight level of disorganised approach, therefore allowing many hidden assumptions and a lot of theoretical work without any practical correspondence). He also makes the point about some branches of theoretical physics becoming more prone to falling to these errors (having many hidden assumptions, hyperfocus and a lot of theoretical work) -- especially quantum physics and, to a lesser extent, statistical physics.

3.b.3 Universality
- Is the derived theory actually universal?
    - Shafer + Colyvan: Which logic do you use and why?
        - Homer example: Was Achilles considered the fastest among mortals by the Greeks? This follows a completely different logic than propositional logic; so other logics clearly possible
        - There is a huge difference between different logics, like ontological (concerned with `being') and epistemological (concerned with `knowing') logic [STANFORD EPISTEMOLOGY, STANFORD ONTOLOGY]
            - In ontology, things are generally seen as ``true'' if they are ``real in the world'' or, in the ever-present scientific tradition of logical positivism, ``are observable or logically deductible''. This view is natural and useful, if one deals with directly observable and/or logical things and/or a few interacting persons or agents – as is the case in a lot of branches of mathematics, biology, chemistry and physics.
            - In epistemology deals with the realm of knowledge, defining something as ``true'' more as ``true to someone''
            - The logic used by Jaynes is a classical, ontological one -- and, thus, epistemics are not treated as first-class citizens inside this theory -- although his work should naturally have been done using an epistemological one
        - As per Van Horn: Important to distinguish fuzzy logic from probability theory, one deals with degree of thruthness, all information being available, other with degree of certainty on a clearly defined decidable-/yes-no-question. Therefore, fuzzy statements (Jim is tall), cannot be solved properly with this type of logic, and therefore also not by this type of probability theory 
        - There is even a fierce, current discussion among physicists which type of logic is the appropriate one to use for describing their theories [PHYSICS]
        - This work therefore uses an epistemological logic as a basis and lays to rest the claims of absoluteness or universality, knowing that yet other logics are perfectly viable
    - There seems to be other problems with propositional logic and the derived probability theory:
        - Weatherson + Colyvan give reason for something not happening as flawed in propositional logic, however has hidden proposition "This event actually happening" -- if that is included, the expected value actually corresponds nicely Expected_value = p(event) * event_valuation = p(event_value & event_actually_happening) * event_valuation = p(event_value | event_actually_happening) * p(event_actually_happening) * event_valuation, in Weatherson's example p(event_actually_happening) is non-negligibly not equal to 1
            - However, having hidden assumptions falisifed happens a lot in research, should a theory maybe account for this before failing? 
                - This happens naturely in an epistemological setting, since there the matter of something happening/being becomes less relevant
        - Being un/certain about the uncertainty itself seems ill-defined, but can be solved in many cases at second glance
            - Coin flip of new coin versus coin flip of throughly tested coin
                - Solved by confidence intervals of posterior
            - Die even versus "is this dog a terrier?" -> Die even versus "is this dog a terrier, a bulldog or something else?" (problem with clearly defining priors)
                - Solved by being precise in meaning and comparing certainty of different events

3.b.4 Subjectivity
- Is the derived theory actually subjective?
    - This is the starkest philosophical contrast between general praxis in the exact sciences Jaynes' theory 
        - Mathematicians and physicists generally follow Parmenides and Plato in allowing for a single truth and/or a `true' Nature (Discovering the inner workings of mathematical theory of Nature as a way to God/Truth; a Neo-Platonic Nature as a true reflection of God)
        - Since scientists see themselves as `uncovering this one single Truth', they like to describe their activities as `objective'; practicing ontology or logical positivism. Since they only deal with a small amount of people and on small domains, this generally doesn't give problems
            - This also reflects the origin of modern science, where doing science was often a lonely enterprise.
        - In this view, uncertainty is often seen as an apriori property of the objects themselves -- a coin is intrinsically described having probabilities 1/2 on each side 
        - In this view, one just needs to repeat an experiment many times to uncover the `true probability' of such an object [approaching certainty with many experiments]
        - This corresponds to what is generally known as the `frequentists' view of probability
        - Jaynes ridicules this view in several passages
            - He demonstrates that this is clearly erroneous as a coin can in an experiment be flipped as to have any probability you like [thus, this view is clearly made up by mathematicians never doing experiments, but just hypothesizing about them -- see the theoretical objection by him above]
            - He does not accept that an experiment can be simply described as `random' as to ignore this problem, since this just hides the precise procedure what entails `random' [see the hidden assumptions objection by him above]
            - Jaynes therefore poses that probability should not be sought in the objects themselves, but in the precise procedures and in the description of information someone has/hasn't; proposing to base probability theory on epistemology
            - This corresponds to the Bayesian view of probability
            - Some theories have inherent uncertainties in them, like statistical physics and quantum physics, and not seeing their probabilities as missing information, but as fundamentally unknown (as in: Given as unknown in Nature itself) gives a misunderstanding of what these theories reflect -- see quantum theory not progressing significantly and the utter confusion around glasses (Jaynes mentions the `projection fallacy', where personal missing information is projected on a situation as a whole or is seen as being fundamentally missing)
        - Note that subjectivity is actually nowhere explicitly introduced in Jaynes' theory, so it is a post-hoc interpretation rather than something inherently represented in the theory itself
        - In this work, an epistemological logic is taken as a basis for the derivations, allowing for baking in subjectivity at a fundamental level
    - Colyvan: Interest is topic not being currently discussed in science, but it should be, since it makes statements non-decidable. Next to true/false you now add an option "I really don't care". Classical logic/domains assume you have intrinsic interest in all of them 
        - This is a not problem when dealing with people that operate on completely different domains 
            - I don't really care whether the ancient Greeks considered Achilles the fastest among mortals 
        - However, this becomes absolutely crucial for overlapping domains. 
            - I don't care for most diseases, except when I would get them -- and even then, I'd care mostly about the results and perhaps some causes [as to prevent it from worsening or (reoccurring) in (people around) me], but not about (bio)chemistry, the organizational structure of the hospital or the inner workings of some medical devices, which are all in fact much related to my newly contracted disease
            - I need a way to deal with people with other domains of interest (also in scientific research)
                - Solution would be to add dimension to probability mapping, making it two-dimensional; Add rules on how to deal with probabilities with different interest values
                    - Adds complexity and only worsens the problem of clearly defining prior information, as it extends to defining prior interest as well
                - Do all operations, like marginalizing/integrating out variables, or rescaling of variables, in the codomain of the probability map -- this does not explicitly account for these difference in interest, but does allow for the consequences
                    - Adds complexity and one always needs all information to bridge the domains, which might not always be at hand
                - Use different logic that allows for adding different values than only true/false to statements
                    - This is done in model-based AI and Clingo; a three- or multiple-valued logic that adds such a state of knowledge explicitly
        - This critique can be repeated for some fact that was known but is now forgotten
            - Classical logic assumes that information is never lost in the process
        - Interest and forgetting and hypothesizing and other knowledge-related phenomena will be explicitly accounted for in this epistemological logic 

3.b.5 Causality
- Does the theory account for causality?
    - It does not, as there is no way to fundamentally define whether wet grass causes rain with this theory [also greatly diminishing claims for universality]
    - Pearl’s framework provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for distinguishing correlation from causation and moves beyond passive observations to active interventions and counterfactual reasoning [CAUSAL NETWORKS -- RELEVANT PAPERS/SOURCES]
        - It has three levels of abstraction:
            - 1. Association. Concerns observational queries of the form P (Y |X ), derived directly from data distributions without causal assumptions except the Causal Markov Condition, which posits that each variable is independent of its non-descendants given its parents in the causal diagram.
            - 2. Intervention. Addresses queries involving the do-operator, written as P (Y |d o(X = x)), representing the distribution of Y under an external manipulation that sets X = x; learning these effects from data requires the Faithfulness Assumption (that observed conditional independencies reflect the graph structure) and other constraints expounded on below.
            - 3. Counterfactuals. Involves reasoning about specific instances under hypothetical conditions, denoted Y x (u), asking what the outcome Y would have been for unit u had X been x; this level demands the strongest commitments regarding the underlying graph and functions.
        - Uses three rules:
            - Rule 1 (Insertion/Deletion of Observations): P (y | d o(x), z, w) = P (y | d o(x), w) if (Y ⊥⊥ Z | X ,W )G X
            - Rule 2 (Action/Observation Exchange): P (y | d o(x), d o(z), w) = P (y | d o(x), z, w) if (Y ⊥⊥ Z | X ,W )G X Z
            - Rule 3 (Insertion/Deletion of Actions): P (y | d o(x), d o(z), w) = P (y | d o(x), w) if (Y ⊥⊥ Z | X ,W )G X Z (W )
        - For the third level (counterfactuals) it uses three steps:
            - 1. Abduction: Where the posterior distribution of exogenous variables P (U |e) is com- puted given observed evidence e to infer the specific state of unit u.
            - 2. Action: Where the structural equation for X is replaced by the constant assignment X = x to create an adjusted model Mx . 11P
            - 3. Prediction: Where the outcome is computed as P (Y x = y|e) = u P (Y x (u) = y|U = u)P (U = u|e). This specification ensures the Consistency Axiom holds (X = x =⇒ Y x = Y ) and guarantees that the mechanism f i remains invariant across factual and counterfactual worlds, allowing for determination of specific noise terms.
        - Explicate what mathematical models are needed at each level, explain what they are, but put every extensive definition in the appendix:
            - Level 1+2: Causal Bayesian networks/graphs
            - Level 3: Structural causal models 
        - Pearl's theory equips (AI) systems with the ability to understand not just what happens, but why it happens and what would happen if the world were different, addressing a fundamental limitation of traditional statistical methods -- including Jaynes' theory
        - Show that Pearl's framework is complete with respect to its rules [CAUSAL NETWORKS -- RELEVANT PAPERS]
        - Pearl is not derived from philosophical foundations, and therefore is done rather post-hoc. There is urgency and the critique feels justified, but there is actually very little philosophical and mathematical/logical foundation to it. it shows how causal reasoning can be formalized, but less fully why causal reasoning is indispensable to human scientific practice.
            - This work acknowledges the necessity of needing a way to communicate about causality; Pearl certainly isn't the only framework [BOOK OF WHY], but it turns out to be hugely convenient and rather general
            - This work will incorporate causality as to allow for humans to meaningfully interact with the encompassed theory and interchange ideas with it; the argument is laid down in {CAUSALITY-ARGUMENT}
        - Usually, Pearl only takes one static kind of causality into account
            - Pearl’s basic SCM formalism usually fixes a model during a query
            - It does not naturally account for causality that changes during the experiment/reasons that change while observing, as the structures/graphs that Pearl uses are rather static once an experiment is started
            - In this work, the structures will be very flexible and allowed to change at will -- supporting an even broader view on causality

5.a Context manipulation in Martin-Löf-style type theory
The following operations are natively allowed in Martin-Löf-style type theory:
- adding a variable
- letting a variable depend on a variable 'earlier' in a context
- swapping variables (if order of context allows)
- deleting variables (if nothing depends on it, via strengthening)
Therefore, the type theory, with some additional definitions and operations done later, accounts for:
- interest: Everything that is in scope can be used, everything that is out of scope can literally not be conversed about. The only appropriate answer when someone refers to proofs or types that are out of scope are "I don't care" or "I don't know"
- learning: Adding variables to the context can directly be seen as learning new statements/proofs and types
- forgetting: When variables are unrelated to anything else, one can remove the variable from the context; This corresponds to forgetting
- causing: this corresponds to setting variables to a value and removing other influences from it; This corresponds to setting a variable and removing relations to that variable
- hypothesizing: This corresponds to a combination of causing, and 'learning' the same variables in an alternative reality; This can be seen as a combination of causing and learning
Therefore, all the operations for epistemic concepts are natively present inside of Martin-Löf-style type theory, but to distinguish it from other operations and encode is more explicitly as transitions between 'epistemics'/'epistemic states', it is more fruitful to classify these operations as 'special' and differences between epistemics as changes between 'modes' (like Kripke worlds).

{CAUSALITY-ARGUMENT}
- Humans think/talk/do completely causally about things/the world/each other and science can be seen as discovering new causal relations in surroundings/environment (be it human-world/society, be it animal/biological world, be it material world) [reasons for this need not be uncovered, this is a mere observation]
- Solution: Huizinga (there is a playful and conjunctive element in all these human activities -- like science; it is therefore a creative ("scheppend") activity done together), Erasmus (there is certainly a degree of irony in anything that is conjured this way/together), Kierkegaard (but this irony is bounded by tradition/everything that is already there/past) AND me (everything is bounded by the fact that it is an activity together, so it needs to go through other people too -- which is flexible (post-truth/manipulation) but like [Kierkegaard's] tradition not infinitely so) [FLECK: Causality is not merely an individual cognitive habit. It is stabilized within scientific communities, inherited styles of thought, shared techniques, and historically formed expectations.] [HACKING: Science is not only a representational activity but an interventionist one. Causal reasoning is central because scientists do things: isolate, manipulate, produce, measure, and stabilize phenomena. Therefore, Pearl's framework is indispensable.]
- This very practically does not mean that any causal story is acceptable. Scientific inquiry is a communal, historically situated practice. With Huizinga, it contains a playful and creative element: scientists construct models, concepts, and experimental situations together. With Erasmus, such constructions retain an ironic or provisional character: they are humanly made and therefore never identical with reality itself. With Kierkegaard, however, irony is not limitless; it is bounded by what has already been inherited. To this I add that scientific construction is also bounded by intersubjective accountability: claims must pass through other people, practices, institutions, instruments, and traditions of criticism.
- Therefore: Since causality is deeply traditional and human, not any observation goes (as according to Feyerabend), and is it completely useful and justified to talk about it (contrary to what a skeptical reading of Hume does) and whether it stems from the `world itself' or `human perspective' becomes irrelevant once science is perceived as inter-human activity (as according to Kant who saw every observation as deeply human -- where causality comes from in this process is completely irrelevant as a justification for this process); any theory that serves the tradition of science should therefore account for causality; Both from pragmatic necessity as well as acknowledgment of the fact that science is after all a deeply human activity and humans have this fundamental need for causal reasoning [see quote Pfeijffer]
- Thus, this interpretation is against a crude Feyerabendian reading, not anything goes ( but a rather 'soft', pluralistic view is allowed for here). Against a crude Humean skepticism, causal talk need not be metaphysically guaranteed in order to be scientifically indispensable. And against a purely Kantian framing, the question whether causality originates in the world or in human cognition becomes secondary once science is understood as a human practice of shared orientation, intervention, and explanation.


{PHYSICS-ARGUMENT}
In [GISIN + QUANTA LOSING INFINITY], there is a helpful suggestion that observations are limited by finite information, and should, naturally, be composed of finite numbers. On a finite-information picture, measurement can actualize new information, the future can be genuinely open, and one can recover analogues of quantum-style indeterminism, measurement-problem-like issues, Wigner’s-friend-like scenarios, no-cloning of indeterminate states, and some entanglement-like correlations. But this still does not recover the genuinely quantum core. Gisin’s indeterministic classical physics lacks incompatible observables in the quantum sense, and therefore does not yield Bell violations, contextuality, the ℏ-structured uncertainty relations, or full quantum entanglement. 

[GISIN] then proposes to base the observed numbers on intuitionism. Although in principle a good suggestion, [GISIN] then, in fact, continues to *not* do that. He continues to base numbers on infinite sequences of 'propensities', which are 'probability-like' numbers, where every probability in this case indicates the possible numeric value on that position. Some values measured would have propensity 1 for the measure value, some values on the edge of the precision of the measurement, would have some higher propensity for some values; the values beyond would have propensities that indicate absolute undertainty about their precise value. This, as [GISIN] indicates, allows numbers to have finite information, but still be 'developed', in principle, ad infinitum.

Albeit charming, this is not the proper interpretation of intuitionism. Firstly, intuitionism is, as the name indicates, something primarily focused on the intuition and, therefore, very epistemological. However, being a classically trained physicist, [GISIN et. al] insist to define these numbers ontologically. Secondly, intuitionism is part of constructive mathematics, so that the numbers are constructable and constructed. In [GISIN]s reading, the number *look* constructed, but are in fact *not* constructed, since the *infinite* sequence of proposensities is, in fact, 'God-given'. Unknowingly, infinity is then introduced in the back-door, exactly contrary to the whole philosophy of constructivism. 

Firstly, the epistemological part: [SPEKKENS et al] already investigated whether a purely epistemological reading of quantum physics would be consistent. His toy theory gives analogues of interference, noncommuting measurements, no-cloning, no-broadcasting, teleportation, dense coding, steering, entanglement-like structure, monogamy, and measurement update as epistemic update. The lesson is that many features commonly advertised as distinctively quantum are not, by themselves, enough to force a radically nonclassical ontology. But the toy theory does not recover full quantum mechanics: it cannot reproduce Bell inequality violations, Kochen-Specker/contextuality phenomena, or the exact Hilbert-space/Born-rule structure. Furthermore, [PUSEY] indicates: It can't get you all the way if you assume a completely certain, completely defined, ontological state underneath. This is hopeful, as it allows for space if some of these constraints are loosened somewhat.

Secondly, the constructive part: The act of measuring a quantity could very naturally be interpreted in this mathematics as 'constructing the measured number', with constructed also implying: Up to the precision of the measurement (for example: In experimental notation .13(4), where .13 is the observed value, and (4) is the +/- 4 in the last digit that the measurement is having). In this reading, there is no need for propensities, all numbers are -- per definition -- always finite, as all measurements are, and all measurements can be seen as developing numbers. 

The natural conjunction of the above is the following: Theories such as a particular logic, are most naturally taken as an epistemological vehicle for describing the encountered observations. There is no need at all to make any assumptions on the ontological aspects, and agnosticism in this regards seems justified. Furthermore, as measurements are finite, the natural choice of representing them is with a constructive logic. The natural mixture of a constructive mathematics that is epistemological in nature, would be intuitionism (so, intuitively -- pun intended -- [GISIN] did make the right choice in that regard). Having a finite mathematics allows for [GISIN], having an epistemological reading allows for [SPEKKENS], whilst not committing to a specific ontology circumvents [PUSEY] and allows to encapsulate standard quantum phenomena both of them are not able to account for (Bell inequality violations, Kochen-Specker/contextuality phenomena, the Born rule, or the ℏ-based incompatibility of observables). This reading also allows better for having incorrect theories and measurements, which an ontological reading of measurements such as [GISIN] would have a hard time to justify.
